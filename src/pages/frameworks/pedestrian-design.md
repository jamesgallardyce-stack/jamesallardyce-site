\## Summary



Pedestrian safety cannot depend on perfect judgment by either drivers or people walking. Streets should be designed so ordinary behavior produces safe outcomes, even when attention is imperfect. Crossings and intersections should be self-enforcing: geometry, speed, and visibility must communicate pedestrian priority, with signage serving as reinforcement rather than the primary mechanism.



\## Crossing priority must be designed, not requested



Pedestrian crossing signs are a warning, not a safety system. When crossings rely on signage alone, the street often continues to operate like a vehicle-priority corridor, placing the burden on pedestrians to negotiate for space. A safer approach is to make the crossing physically obvious and mechanically credible through raised crosswalks, shorter crossing distances, tighter turning radii, improved sightlines, and consistent lighting.



\## Why sign-only crossings fail in practice



Sign-only crossings frequently produce two outcomes that degrade the pedestrian experience:



1\. Pedestrians hesitate and wait for drivers to pass; some drivers interpret that hesitation as permission to continue through, reinforcing delay and uncertainty.

2\. When pedestrians use the crossing as intended, some drivers misread the action as improper or unexpected, respond aggressively, or fail to yield—creating conflict and, in the worst cases, collisions.



This is a design failure: the street is sending mixed messages about who has priority.



\## Minimum standard for a safe crossing



\- Target speed is low enough that mistakes are survivable.

\- Crossing distance is short and sightlines are clear.

\- Turning movements are slowed by geometry.

\- The crossing is visually obvious and physically credible.

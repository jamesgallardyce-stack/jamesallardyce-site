## Design for good people, not perfect behavior

Street design should be grounded in how good people actually behave, not in the assumption of perfect driving. There will always be reckless drivers, but most harm on our streets is caused by ordinary, well-intentioned people making momentary mistakes behind the wheel. In a car-dominant system, those mistakes can cost others their lives, limbs, or long-term mobility. Streets should therefore be designed to protect pedestrians, cyclists, and pets from the consequences of human error, using geometry, speed management, and clear separation of modes. Designing for good people—by acknowledging our limits rather than denying them—creates safer streets for everyone.

## The opportunity cost of car-first design

Every transportation mode has real tradeoffs and failure modes, and no system is free. The issue is that the opportunity cost of car-first infrastructure is unusually high: it consumes land, increases exposure to severe injury, and imposes daily barriers on people who are not driving—especially older adults and people with mobility constraints. When streets are designed around vehicle speed and throughput, the “cost” is paid in longer crossings, missing curb access, higher crash severity, and reduced independence.

People using electric wheelchairs and other mobility devices can preserve independence when sidewalks are continuous, crossings are safe and frequent, and transit access is reliable. The same design changes that protect them—lower speeds, clearer crossings, protected space for biking and transit, and predictable geometry—also make streets safer and more comfortable for everyone. Streets designed for people produce better public life: more walking, more local activity, and a city that feels calmer and more humane.

Some interventions can seem counterintuitive at first, especially when they challenge familiar driving habits. But we cannot afford to spend years waiting for consensus while preventable injuries and deaths continue. Evidence-based street design can save lives now, and education can follow implementation—not the other way around.


## Residential streets

Residential streets should prioritize safety, predictability, and livability over throughput. These streets serve daily life, not regional travel. Design should assume frequent pedestrian activity and occasional driver error, using low speeds, narrow travel lanes, and clear cues that reduce the severity of mistakes.

## Mixed-use streets

Mixed-use streets require balance because they host the widest range of users. People are walking, cycling, loading vehicles, visiting businesses, and driving through the same space. Design should slow vehicles to human-scale speeds, separate modes where possible, and make pedestrian movement intuitive and visible.

## Business corridors

Business corridors succeed when people feel comfortable arriving and staying, not just passing through. Streets that prioritize fast vehicle movement discourage foot traffic and weaken local commerce. Design should support frequent crossings, short walking distances, safe curb access, and reliable transit movement.

## Arterials and through routes

Through routes still exist within cities, but design must manage speed and conflict points to reduce the consequences of inevitable mistakes. Predictable geometry, access management, and transit priority can move people efficiently without turning streets into barriers or danger zones.

## Pedestrian street design

Pedestrian safety cannot depend on perfect judgment by either drivers or people walking. Streets should be designed so that ordinary behavior produces safe outcomes, even when attention is imperfect. In practice, this means designing crossings and intersections to be self-enforcing: the street geometry, speed, and visibility should communicate that people walking have priority, with signage serving as reinforcement rather than the primary control.

### Crossing priority must be designed, not requested

Pedestrian crossing signs are a warning, not a safety system. When crossings rely on signage alone, the street often continues to operate like a vehicle-priority corridor, placing the burden on pedestrians to negotiate for space. A safer approach is to make the crossing physically obvious and mechanically credible through raised crosswalks, shorter crossing distances, tighter turning radii, improved sightlines, and consistent lighting. These treatments reduce speed and ambiguity, which is what prevents crashes.

### Why sign-only crossings often fail

Sign-only crossings frequently create two outcomes that both degrade the pedestrian experience. First, pedestrians hesitate and wait for drivers to pass, hoping a gap will open; some drivers interpret that hesitation as confirmation that they should continue through, which prolongs delay and reinforces uncertainty. Second, when pedestrians use the crossing as intended, some drivers misread the action as improper or unexpected, respond aggressively, or fail to yield—creating conflict and, in the worst cases, collisions. This is a design failure: the street is sending mixed messages about who has priority.

### Principle

If a location functions as a pedestrian crossing, the street should be built to behave like one. The goal is to reduce negotiation and surprise by making pedestrian movement predictable and protected, and making yielding the default driver behavior.
